Scope gantry

A soldering microscope that hangs under the shelf above my bench and slides to wherever the board is. Two linear rails, three lengths of aluminum extrusion and a handful of printed parts. It's still being built, so this page is too.

Year
2026, in progress
Made with
An Andonstar AD246S-M microscope, MGN12 linear rails, 2020 aluminum extrusion, OpenSCAD, PLA, one size of screw
I did
The measuring, drilling, printing and assembly. The design and CAD came out of a long back-and-forth with Claude
Links
Printed parts and source
Why

I inspect boards under a cheap digital microscope on a stand. I wanted it to reach any spot on the bench and to be out of the way the rest of the time.

The bottom shelf over the bench has 14.5 inches of air under it. So the scope hangs there, on a gantry that covers the bench left to right and most of it front to back, and the bench stays clear.

How it works

One precision rail runs along the front lip of the shelf. A second, plain rail runs along the back, between the uprights. A short boom bridges the two and slides left and right. A rail under the boom carries the microscope front to back.

Rail on the shelf lip→Boom→Rail under the boom→Rod clamp→Microscope

The front rail does all the guiding. The back one only holds the boom's far end at the right height, on two strips of slippery tape, with a millimeter of play front to back. Two precision rails a meter and a half long would have to be parallel to a fraction of a millimeter. One rail and one slider don't.

The numbers it had to fit

Under the shelf14.5 in, bench top to the bottom of the shelf's lip
Shelf57 in wide, 12 in deep, set back over a bench 25 in deep
Side to sideAbout 55 in of travel on a 1450 mm rail
Front to backThe lens reaches from about 8 to 21 in from the back of the bench
Lens to boardAbout 177 mm parked, 147 mm with the head wound down
Tightest clearance2.5 mm, between the top of the screen and the bracket it passes under

Borrowed from the microscope

The scope's stand came with a 20 mm rod with a flat on one side, a block that rides it, and a fine height adjust with 30 mm of travel. All three moved to the gantry. The rod slides through a printed clamp for extra reach, and the height adjust is the whole Z axis.

The screen flips up and sits beside the lens, which is the only way the head fits under the shelf with room left to focus.

The design

Clamps, then bolts

The first plan held the front rail on with seven printed clamps hooked around the shelf's lip, so nothing got drilled. They would have worked. They also hung 7.5 mm below the lip, sat under tension forever, and put plastic between the shelf and the one rail that has to stay straight.

Eight small holes through the lip replaced all seven. The extrusion bolts straight to the steel, and the holes are hidden behind it.

The back rail went where the bolts were

Each end of the shelf hangs from its upright on two bolts. The back rail is tapped at both ends and held by the top bolt on each side, so the shelf's own hardware carries it. That decided the rail's height, which decided where the boom had to stop, which changed the slider. A printed collar at each end keeps the rail from turning on its single bolt.

One size of screw

Everything printed is drawn around an M3 × 10 socket head screw: wall thicknesses, counterbores and how far each screw reaches into a T-nut. The two exceptions are the quarter-inch bolts that hold the back rail, because those were the taps I had.

What it's taught me so far
  1. 1

    Ask where the top ends up

    How tall the head is matters less than where its top edge lands once it's in focus.

  2. 2

    Let the existing holes decide

    The shelf's own bolts turned out to be the best place to hang the back rail.

  3. 3

    If you can drill it, bolt it

    Seven clever clamps lost to eight plain holes.

  4. 4

    Print the gauge before the part

    A ten-minute ring with three bores picked the fit for the rod clamp.

  5. 5

    Pick one screw

    Designing every part around the same fastener is less work than sorting a bin later.

Carriage bracketJoins the front carriage to the boom by going under the shelf's lip. STL OpenSCAD
Back sliderWraps the back rail and grips the boom's far end. Two pieces. STL OpenSCAD
Rod clampA D-shaped bore and a thumbscrew, bolted under the second carriage. STL OpenSCAD
Rail collarsLeft and right. They stop the back rail turning on its bolts. STL OpenSCAD
Lip spacersEight flat ones, so a 10 mm screw doesn't bottom out in the extrusion. STL OpenSCAD
Rod fit gaugeThree bores, three clearances. Print this first. STL OpenSCAD

Heads up: these are working files for a build that isn't finished, and they're sized to my shelf and my microscope. The dimensions are variables at the top of each OpenSCAD file.

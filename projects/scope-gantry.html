Scope gantry

A soldering microscope that hangs under the shelf above my bench and slides to wherever the board is. Two linear rails, three lengths of aluminum extrusion and a handful of printed parts. The rails are still in the mail, so the electronics got built first: a ring light, and a Raspberry Pi that puts the view on a web page. It's still being built, so this page is too.

Why

I inspect boards under a cheap digital microscope on a stand. I wanted it to reach any spot on the bench and to be out of the way the rest of the time.

The bottom shelf over the bench has 14.5 inches of air under it. So the scope hangs there, on a gantry that covers the bench left to right and most of it front to back, and the bench stays clear.

How it works

One precision rail runs along the front lip of the shelf. A second, plain rail runs along the back, between the uprights. A short boom bridges the two and slides left and right. A rail under the boom carries the microscope front to back.

Rail on the shelf lip→Boom→Rail under the boom→Rod clamp→Microscope

The front rail does all the guiding. The back one only holds the boom's far end at the right height, on two strips of slippery tape, with a millimeter of play front to back. Two precision rails a meter and a half long would have to be parallel to a fraction of a millimeter. One rail and one slider don't.

The numbers it had to fit

Shelf57 in wide, 12 in deep, set back over a bench 25 in deep
Side to sideAbout 55 in of travel on a 1450 mm rail
Front to backThe lens reaches from about 8 to 21 in from the back of the bench
Lens to boardAbout 177 mm parked, 147 mm with the head wound down
Tightest clearance2.5 mm, between the top of the screen and the bracket it passes under

Borrowed from the microscope

The scope's stand came with a 20 mm rod with a flat on one side, a block that rides it, and a fine height adjust with 30 mm of travel. All three moved to the gantry. The rod slides through a printed clamp for extra reach, and the height adjust is the whole Z axis.

The screen flips up and sits beside the lens, which is the only way the head fits under the shelf with room left to focus.

Lights and a web page

The scope's own lights sit in the head, a long way from the board once it hangs under a shelf. So a ring of twelve LEDs rides on the end of the lens, in a printed sleeve held by the two thumbscrews that already hold the lens on.

A Raspberry Pi 3 B lives on the back of the screen. It powers the scope over USB, drives the ring, and serves one page with the live view and every control on it. The page is a single Python file with nothing to install but the LED library. MediaMTX carries the video.

Ring lightBrightness, how many LEDs are lit, all round or from one side, which side, and warm, cool or any color
MicroscopeOn and off, by switching the Pi's USB power. A Pi 3 B switches all four ports together
Save a frameStops the live view, takes a 1920 × 1080 still straight from the scope, and starts the view again
Turned 180°The scope hangs upside down from where its screen expects to be, so the page and the stills turn to match
Health lineSupply voltage, Pi temperature and Wi-Fi signal, in amber when one is off

A small live view and big stills

The scope sends about 6 megabits of video a second at 1080p, and the same at 720p. That was more than the Pi's 2.4 GHz radio could carry here. The picture froze, and every command waited seconds behind it. At 640 × 480 the scope sends about half that, the freezes stop, and a command lands in about 15 milliseconds.

So the live view is small. I position by the scope's own screen anyway, and the page is there to show the lighting change. Stills don't go through the stream at all.

The design

Clamps, then bolts

The first plan held the front rail on with seven printed clamps hooked around the shelf's lip, so nothing got drilled. They would have worked. They also hung 7.5 mm below the lip, sat under tension forever, and put plastic between the shelf and the one rail that has to stay straight.

Eight small holes through the lip replaced all seven. The extrusion bolts straight to the steel, and the holes are hidden behind it.

The back rail went where the bolts were

Each end of the shelf hangs from its upright on two bolts. The back rail is tapped at both ends and held by the top bolt on each side, so the shelf's own hardware carries it. That decided the rail's height, which decided where the boom had to stop, which changed the slider. A printed collar at each end keeps the rail from turning on its single bolt.

One size of screw

Everything printed is drawn around an M3 × 10 socket head screw: wall thicknesses, counterbores and how far each screw reaches into a T-nut. The two exceptions are the quarter-inch bolts that hold the back rail, because those were the taps I had.

The chain hides behind the shelf

The power lead has to follow the head across 55 inches. A cable chain does that, and the first two layouts put it on top of the shelf. The shelf's back edge turns up 45 mm, and behind that lip is dead space. The chain lies on its side there. One end hangs on a printed clip on the lip and the other bolts to an arm on the back slider.

The clips hook over the lip and pinch it with a screw, so nothing new got drilled. The drawing had left and right swapped until the printed parts said otherwise.

Four homes for the Pi

The Pi was drawn clipped across the screen, hung under the rod clamp, standing on edge off the hinge, and bolted to the screen's own case screws. The last one needed 20 mm M2 screws the hardware store didn't have. It ended up on a printed plate with the nylon bolts from its old case, stuck to the back of the screen with Velcro.

What it's taught me so far
  1. 1

    Ask where the top ends up

    How tall the head is matters less than where its top edge lands once it's in focus.

  2. 2

    Let the existing holes decide

    The shelf's own bolts turned out to be the best place to hang the back rail.

  3. 3

    If you can drill it, bolt it

    Seven clever clamps lost to eight plain holes.

  4. 4

    Print the gauge before the part

    A ten-minute ring with three bores picked the fit for the rod clamp.

  5. 5

    Pick one screw

    Designing every part around the same fastener is less work than sorting a bin later.

  6. 6

    Measure the volts at the far end

    Twice, a laggy page turned out to be thin wires starving the Pi, not the network.

Ring light sleeveSlides over the lens and holds the LED ring, with an optional diffuser cap. Sleeve Cap OpenSCAD
Pi plateA flat back for a Raspberry Pi 3 B, with the bolt heads sunk so Velcro sits flat. STL OpenSCAD
Control pageOne Python file for the Pi: the page, the ring, the USB switch and the stills. scope_ui.py
Rod fit gaugeThree bores, three clearances. Print this first. STL OpenSCAD

Heads up: these are working files for a build that isn't finished, and they're sized to my shelf and my microscope. The dimensions are variables at the top of each OpenSCAD file. The control page expects MediaMTX serving the scope at a path called scope, and it has to run as root to drive the LEDs.
